# Voice and tone

Steven Needham’s voice is calm, precise, systems-minded, and grounded in
operational evidence. It explains how conditions, people, tools, and decisions
connect without turning that work into promotional language.

## Core voice

### Calm

Use direct, unhurried language. Confidence comes from specificity and clear
reasoning—not urgency, exaggeration, or enthusiasm cues.

Prefer:

> The workflow turns uneven public reports into a prioritized field list.

Avoid:

> This game-changing workflow revolutionizes service response!

### Concrete

Name the actual work, scale, timeframe, users, constraints, and result. Use
specific numbers when they are supported, and honest approximation when they
are not.

Prefer:

> Analyzed roughly six months and 20,000+ rides to identify persistent fleet
> underperformance.

Avoid:

> Analyzed extensive mobility data to unlock powerful insights.

### Evidence-led

State what is known, how it was learned, what remains uncertain, and what the
evidence supports. Separate observed conditions from inference.

Prefer:

> Provider-reported availability suggests a persistent supply gap; trip demand
> and operator intent are not represented in this feed.

Avoid:

> The data proves the neighborhood is underserved.

### Human and systems-aware

Describe systems as environments used by people. Explain operational effects
without reducing the work to software features or abstract process language.

Prefer:

> The scan record gives warehouse and field leads a shared account of custody
> without slowing the handoff.

Avoid:

> The solution optimizes asset-management synergies.

## Sentence and content patterns

- Use sentence case for headings and body copy.
- Reserve uppercase for short mono labels, eyebrows, statuses, and metadata.
- Prefer active verbs and named subjects.
- Use one dense, precise explanatory sentence rather than several promotional
  fragments.
- Pair conclusions with evidence, method, or limitations.
- Use first person for mission and considered perspective.
- Use third person where a portfolio biography needs editorial distance.
- Use arrows only as functional link cues or process notation.
- Do not use emoji.
- Avoid exclamation points.
- Avoid unsupported superlatives: best, groundbreaking, revolutionary,
  world-class, transformative.
- Avoid vague technology claims: AI-powered, smart, seamless, innovative.
- Do not hide uncertainty behind polished language.

## Tone by context

| Context | Tone |
|---|---|
| Portfolio mission | Personal, declarative, concise |
| Case study | Analytical, specific, transparent about role and limits |
| Public mobility content | Educational, organization-neutral, civic-minded |
| Consulting proposal | Candid, practical, outcome-aware, never manipulative |
| Status report | Brief, accountable, explicit about movement and blockers |
| Agreement or SOW | Plain, precise, unambiguous |
| Presentation | Audience-facing, cumulative, one claim at a time |
| Social post | Observational and useful; avoid engagement bait |

## Status language

Status labels describe operational reality:

- **On track** — expected work and dependencies remain viable.
- **Watch** — a condition may affect delivery but is not yet blocking it.
- **Blocked** — progress requires a specific decision, input, or external
  change.
- **Not started** — work has not begun; this is not inherently negative.

Always pair the status with a sentence explaining why and what happens next.

## Calls to action

Calls to action are invitations to inspect, understand, or continue:

- View the project
- Read the evidence
- Review the architecture
- Download the report
- Continue the conversation

Avoid urgency, scarcity, conversion pressure, and generic promotional buttons.

## Editorial review

Before publishing, ask:

1. Is every important claim supported by evidence or clearly labeled as an
   inference?
2. Does the copy name actual people, conditions, tools, scale, or decisions?
3. Are uncertainty and limitations visible?
4. Could any promotional adjective be replaced with a fact?
5. Does the reader understand what changed, why it matters, and what comes
   next?
6. Is public mobility content organization-neutral unless a specific
   organization was requested?
